Every project we take on starts the same way: two of us, a laser measure, a notebook and most of a morning in your house. Clients are sometimes surprised that we want to measure rooms they have no plans to touch. This post explains why.

## Houses are never quite square

Even a house built last year is a few centimetres out somewhere. A Victorian terrace can be out by much more — walls lean, floors slope towards the back, and the chimney breast on the first floor rarely lines up with the one below. None of this matters until you try to fit a new staircase, a run of kitchen units or a steel beam that has been ordered to the millimetre.

A measured survey records the house as it really is, not as the estate agent's floor plan suggests. That drawing becomes the base for everything that follows.

## What we are really looking for

Measuring is the easy part. While we are there, we are also reading the building:

- **Structure** — which walls carry the floors above, where the joists run, and what is holding up the bay window.
- **Services** — where the drains leave the house, how deep they are, and whether the boiler can cope with another bathroom.
- **History** — earlier extensions, blocked-up doorways and repairs that tell us how the house has been treated.
- **Light** — where the sun reaches at different times of day, which matters far more than most people expect.

> The survey is the cheapest part of the job, and it is the part that saves the most money later on.

## Why the whole house

The room you want to change is connected to every other room. Moving a kitchen to the back of the house might free up the front room as a study, but only if the hallway can be rearranged. Converting the loft depends on where a stair can land on the floor below. We often find the best answer is in a room the client had not mentioned.

Measuring everything also means we only do it once. If the project grows, or you come back to us in five years for the next stage, the drawings are already on file.

## What you get afterwards

Within a week of the visit you will receive a set of scaled plans and sections of the house as it stands, along with a short note on anything we spotted that might affect the project: a drain in an awkward place, a wall that is doing more work than it looks, signs of damp that need attention first.

That note is often the most useful thing we produce, and it comes with every [design package](/services/). It is far better to learn about a problem at the drawing stage, when it costs a conversation, than on site, when it costs a week.
