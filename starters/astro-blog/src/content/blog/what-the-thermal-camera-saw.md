The library lends out a thermal camera, two weeks at a time, with a waiting list. Mine came up in February, which is exactly when you want it: the colder it is outside, the more clearly heat shows where it's escaping.

## Outside, at night

I went round the house at ten in the evening with the heating on and every internal door open. From the street, the picture told the story in one frame: the bay window glowing at every joint, the front door outlined in orange, and a bright band under the eaves along the whole front.

![The bay window close up on the thermal camera, the frames and the joint above the bay brightest.](/images/blog/house-front.webp "In daylight, the same bay. The joint where the bay roof meets the wall turned out to be open to the loft.")

That band under the eaves was the surprise. The loft insulation had been laid years ago and stopped a hand's width from the wall all the way along — leaving a cold strip at the top of every upstairs room.

## Inside, by morning

Inside, the camera found the smaller, sneakier losses: cold air sliding under skirting boards, the gap round the loft hatch, and the letterbox, which on the screen looked like a small window left open.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> A thermal camera shows surfaces, not causes. A cold patch on a wall can be missing insulation, a draught behind it, or damp. Check before you spend money on the answer you assumed.
