Solid nine-inch brick walls have no cavity to fill. You can insulate them on the outside, which changes how the house looks, or on the inside, which costs you a little floor space and a lot of plastering. For the two coldest walls — the back bedroom's gable and the kitchen below it — I went inside.

## Why wood fibre and lime

An old solid wall gets wet from the outside in a storm and dries out both ways. Cover the inside with foil-backed board and gypsum and it can only dry outward; in a wet winter the brick behind the board stays damp, and damp brick is cold brick. Wood-fibre board and lime plaster let the wall breathe to the inside.

```proscons
title: Inside or outside?
prosLabel: Inside (what I did)
consLabel: Outside
pros:
  - Room by room, no scaffolding, no planning questions
  - Keeps the brick front of the house as it is
  - Could be done in the coldest rooms only
cons:
  - Covers the whole wall in one go, without the cold spots at floors and partitions
  - No disruption inside
  - Changes the look of the house — on a 1930s street, that matters
```

## The job

```timeline
title: Four weeks, one room and the kitchen below
items:
  - date: Week 1
    title: Strip back to brick
    text: Old gypsum plaster off, skirting and sockets out, the window board removed.
  - date: Week 2
    title: Boards up
    text: 60 mm wood-fibre boards fixed straight to the brick with mushroom plugs, every joint tight.
  - date: Week 3
    title: Lime plaster, two coats
    text: A base coat with mesh, then a finishing coat. Slow to dry; the dehumidifier ran for days.
  - date: Week 4
    title: Back together
    text: New deeper window board, skirting and sockets moved forward, limewash on top.
```

A warm wall feels different. The room used to feel cold even at 20 °C, because your body loses heat to a cold surface. Now it feels warm at 18 °C, and the thermostat is set lower as a result.
