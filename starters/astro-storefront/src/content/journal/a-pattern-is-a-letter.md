Every coat we make starts as a handful of brown paper shapes. A front, a back, a sleeve, a collar, a facing, a pocket bag. Lay them out on the table and they look like nothing at all. Lay them on the cloth, cut round them, and you have a coat in pieces.

## Reading a pattern

I was taught that a pattern is a letter to the person who cuts it. Everything they need to know should be written on it, and nothing else. The grain line, so the cloth hangs straight. The notches, so the sleeve goes into the armhole at the right place. A small circle where the pocket goes. If the cutter has to come and ask me what something means, I have written a bad letter.

## Why we still use paper

We could keep our patterns on a computer and print them. Some of them are, as a backup. But paper has a memory that a file does not. The field coat's back piece has been traced around so many times that its edges are soft, and you can see in pencil every change we have made since 2019: the pocket moved down, the hem let out, the shoulder taken in by three millimetres after a letter from a customer in Haldenmoor.

## The shears

The other thing on the table is a pair of heavy black shears, older than the workroom. Nobody uses them on paper. That is the only rule in the room that has never been broken.
